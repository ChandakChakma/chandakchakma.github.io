Self-Play as a Game: Cycling, Expert Iteration and STaR

Date: November 6, 2026  |  Estimated Reading Time: 5 min  |  Author: Chandak Chakma

Naive self-play can spiral or cycle. Fictitious play, opponent pools and PSRO fix it; expert iteration explains AlphaZero; and STaR turns out to be an EM algorithm for the log-likelihood of the right answer.

12 Games, equilibria, and why self-play cycles

Definitionzero-sum games and equilibria

Player 1 gets payoff \(u(a,b)\) and player 2 gets \(-u(a,b)\). With mixed strategies \(s,t\) and payoff matrix \(U\), von Neumann’s minimax theorem states \(\max_s\min_t s^\top U t=\min_t\max_s s^\top U t\). A Nash equilibrium is a pair of strategies in which no player gains by changing alone.

Key resultnaive self-play can spiral

On \(f(u,v)=uv\), where \(u\) minimizes and \(v\) maximizes, simultaneous gradient steps \(u_{t+1}=u_t-\eta v_t\), \(v_{t+1}=v_t+\eta u_t\) give \[u_{t+1}^2+v_{t+1}^2=(1+\eta^2)\,(u_t^2+v_t^2),\] so the iterates spiral away from the equilibrium \((0,0)\) for any step size.

In rock–paper–scissors, best responses cycle forever: rock, then paper, then scissors, then rock. The standard fixes:

  • Fictitious play: best-respond to the average of all past opponents; the time-averages converge in two-player zero-sum games (Robinson, 1951).

  • Opponent pools: play against a random earlier checkpoint, as AlphaGo did.

  • PSRO (Lanctot et al., 2017): grow a population of policies and mix them by a meta-level Nash equilibrium.

  • Optimistic or extragradient updates: these converge on bilinear games where plain gradient steps spiral.

Why it matters

Challenger–Solver self-play is general-sum and non-stationary: each side’s target moves as the other learns. Forgetting earlier skills, the cross-round mode cycling reported by R-Diverse, is the LLM form of this cycling. Memory banks and populations are the LLM form of fictitious play.

13 Search as a teacher: expert iteration and AlphaZero

Expert iteration (Anthony et al., 2017) alternates two steps. An improvement operator \(\mathcal I\), such as tree search, filtering or voting, turns the current policy into a stronger one; then the policy is trained to imitate it, \(\pi\leftarrow\arg\min_\pi D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathcal I(\pi)\,\|\,\pi)\). AlphaZero is the famous case, with loss \[\ell=(z-v)^2-\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathrm{MCTS}}^{\top}\log\mathbf p+c\,\|\theta\|^2 ,\] where \(z\) is the real game result, \(v\) the predicted value, \(\boldsymbol\pi_{\mathrm{MCTS}}\) the search’s move distribution and \(\mathbf p\) the network’s.

Key resultthe condition for self-improvement

The loop improves only if \(\mathcal I(\pi)\) is reliably better than \(\pi\). In Go, search plus the true game result guarantees this. In label-free LLM self-play the operator is usually a majority vote, which is better than the policy only when the mode is right.

14 STaR as maximum marginal likelihood

Key resultFisher’s identity

With a latent rationale \(z\) and gold answer \(y^\star\), \[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(y^\star\mid x)=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_\theta(z\mid x,y^\star)}\Big[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(z,y^\star\mid x)\Big].\]

Derivation. \(\nabla_\theta\log p(y^\star\mid x)=\frac{1}{p(y^\star\mid x)}\sum_z\nabla_\theta p(z,y^\star\mid x)=\sum_z\frac{p(z,y^\star\mid x)}{p(y^\star\mid x)}\nabla_\theta\log p(z,y^\star\mid x)\), and the weight is the posterior \(p(z\mid x,y^\star)\). If the answer is read off the rationale, \(y=\mathrm{ans}(z)\), then sampling \(z\sim p_\theta(z\mid x)\) and keeping it only when \(\mathrm{ans}(z)=y^\star\) gives exact posterior samples. Fine-tuning on the kept rationales, as STaR does (Zelikman et al., 2022), is therefore a stochastic gradient of \(\log p_\theta(y^\star\mid x)\): an EM-style algorithm.

Key resultRL versus likelihood

RL with a \(0/1\) reward maximizes \(p_\theta(y^\star\mid x)\), and \(\nabla_\theta p=p\,\nabla_\theta\log p\). STaR maximizes \(\log p_\theta(y^\star\mid x)\), and \(\nabla_\theta\log p=\nabla_\theta p/p\). On a problem solved \(1\%\) of the time, the likelihood objective weights the gradient \(100\) times more than RL does: log-likelihood favors hard problems.

Why it matters

“Maximize \(p\)” versus “maximize \(\log p\)” decides whether rare successes on hard problems drive learning. It ties directly to the Challenger’s job of keeping \(p\) in the learnable range.

Exercises

Exercise 7. In the bilinear example with \(\eta=0.1\) and start \((u,v)=(1,0)\), how far from the equilibrium are the iterates after \(10\) steps?

Show answer

The squared radius grows by \(1.01\) per step: \(\sqrt{1.01^{10}}=1.051\).

Exercise 8. A problem has \(p_\theta(y^\star\mid x)=0.05\). By what factor does the likelihood gradient exceed the RL gradient on this problem?

Show answer

\(1/p=20\).
