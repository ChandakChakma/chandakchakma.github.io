Where Can New Knowledge Come From? Data Processing and the Verifier Gap

Date: November 6, 2026  |  Estimated Reading Time: 3 min  |  Author: Chandak Chakma

A closed loop without external information cannot create knowledge, only concentrate it. The data processing inequality formalizes this limit, and the generator–verifier gap specifies when filtering one’s own outputs helps.

15 The data processing inequality

Key resultprocessing cannot create information

If \(T\to X\to S\) forms a Markov chain, that is, if the signal \(S\) is computed from \(X\) alone, then \[I(T;S)\le I(T;X).\]

Let \(T\) denote the truth, \(X\) the problem together with the model, and \(S\) any label-free signal computed from the model’s own samples, such as votes, confidence scores, or consistency checks. The inequality states that no such signal carries more information about the truth than the model already holds. Label-free self-play can therefore extract and concentrate this information, but it cannot create it. For the loop to expand what the model knows, it requires an external source of information (an executor, a proof checker, or an environment) or an asymmetry, for instance a task on which checking is easier than solving.

An important qualification. The inequality bounds information, not usability. A model may “know” an answer in the sense of pass@256 and still fail at pass@1. Converting such latent knowledge into reliable behavior has genuine value, but it constitutes sharpening rather than the acquisition of new knowledge.

16 The generator–verifier gap

Key resultfiltering helps only if the check is informative

Suppose self-generated answers are filtered by the model’s own check, with true positive rate \(\mathrm{TPR}=\Pr(\text{accept}\mid\text{correct})\) and false positive rate \(\mathrm{FPR}=\Pr(\text{accept}\mid\text{wrong})\). By Bayes’ rule, the precision, that is, the accuracy among accepted answers, is \[\begin{gathered}\mathrm{prec}=\frac{p\,\mathrm{TPR}}{p\,\mathrm{TPR}+(1-p)\,\mathrm{FPR}}, \\[4pt] \text{and} \\[4pt] \mathrm{prec}>p\iff\mathrm{TPR}>\mathrm{FPR}.\end{gathered}\]

Proof. A direct rearrangement gives \(\mathrm{prec}>p\iff\mathrm{TPR}>p\,\mathrm{TPR}+(1-p)\mathrm{FPR}\iff(1-p)\mathrm{TPR}>(1-p)\mathrm{FPR}\).

Examplean informative and an uninformative verifier

With \(p=0.3\), \(\mathrm{TPR}=0.9\), and \(\mathrm{FPR}=0.2\), we obtain \(\mathrm{prec}=0.27/(0.27+0.14)=0.659\), more than double the base accuracy, so training on the accepted answers can help. If instead \(\mathrm{TPR}=\mathrm{FPR}\), then \(\mathrm{prec}=p\) and filtering provides no benefit.

Song et al. (2025) formalize this generation–verification gap and study how it varies across models and verification methods. Their analysis offers the cleanest characterization of when self-improvement is possible at all.

Why it matters

A majority vote is itself a verifier: its TPR and FPR are determined by how often correct and incorrect answers become the mode. When errors are shared, a wrong answer becomes the mode as readily as a correct one, and the gap closes.

Exercises

Exercise 10. A verifier has \(\mathrm{TPR}=0.8\) and \(\mathrm{FPR}=0.4\) on a problem set with \(p=0.2\). What is the accuracy of the answers it accepts?

Show solution

\(0.16/(0.16+0.32)=0.333\), which exceeds the base rate of \(0.2\); filtering therefore helps.
