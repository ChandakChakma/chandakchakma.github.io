9.1 How large is benchmark noise?#

Key resultBenchmark uncertainty

For a benchmark of \(N\) problems with accuracy near \(p\), the standard error is \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/N}\), and a \(95\%\) confidence interval extends approximately \(\pm1.96\) standard errors around the estimate.

Benchmark Problems \(N\) Standard error at \(p=0.5\) \(95\%\) half-width
AIME (single year) \(30\) \(0.091\) \(\pm 17.9\) points
MATH-500 \(500\) \(0.022\) \(\pm 4.4\) points
GSM8K (test split) \(1{,}319\) \(0.014\) \(\pm 2.7\) points

Averaging \(k\) samples per problem (avg@\(k\)) averages out sampling noise, but it does not address the variance that arises from which problems happen to be included in the benchmark. Two methods should therefore be compared with a paired bootstrap over the same set of problems.

9.2 A checklist before claiming results#

  • Same compute: match the number of rollouts, tokens and optimizer steps for every baseline.

  • Seeds: use at least three seeds for training curves; a “rise, then fall” claim requires the decline to exceed the spread across seeds.

  • pass@\(k\): use the unbiased estimator with \(n\gg k\), and report results up to large \(k\).

  • Novelty: report the base model’s pass@256 and count the problems that the base model never solves.

  • Contamination: check for exact and \(13\)-gram overlap between generated problems and every evaluation benchmark.

  • Decision rules first: specify the pass/fail criterion before the run, and never select checkpoints on test sets.

  • Read the code: published numbers are produced by code, not by equations (Section 3.2).

9.3 Open problems: where a new thread can lead#

  1. Sustained growth. Pure self-play peaks after roughly three to five rounds, and later Challengers are no more instructive than earlier ones (What Actually Evolves in Self-Evolving RL?, ICLR 2027 submission). Collapse mitigations such as R-Diverse and data gating (Survive or Collapse) prevent the decline but not the plateau. Goal: a loop that continues to improve.

  2. Answer keys under shared errors. The goal is a label-free signal that outperforms majority vote in the minority-truth zone (Part 6) by drawing on a channel that a shared misreading does not control (Sections 6.4 and 6.8).

  3. Search with a strict check. The aim is to find rare correct answers and to recognize them without labels. The check’s false-acceptance rate on the majority vote’s incorrect answer must remain below the share of samples that carry the correct answer (Part 7).

  4. Measured expansion. Progress should be demonstrated as newly solved problems at large \(k\), beyond the base model’s pass@256 (Section 2.2), rather than as pass@1 gains that may reflect only sharpening.

  5. Grounded teacher rewards without labels. SOAR shows that rewarding actual student progress is effective, but it requires a labeled set of hard problems (Section 3.2).

  6. A theory of closed-loop improvement. Under what conditions can a loop with no external information improve, and by how much? The data processing inequality (Section 5.1) and the verifier gap (Section 5.2) delineate the boundary.

9.4 A four-week study plan#

Week Study in these notes and derive by hand Primary readings
1 Part 1: policy gradient, baselines, the RLOO identity, PPO clipping Sutton and Barto, ch. 13 [1]; PPO [2]; GRPO [3]; RLOO [4]
2 Part 2: the tilted optimum, pass@\(k\) and its estimator DPO derivation [6]; pass@\(k\) [7]; Yue et al. [8]; Dr. GRPO [5]
3 Part 3: learnability, proposer rewards, noisy labels Absolute Zero [10]; R-Zero [11]; SOAR [12]; PAIRED [9]
4 Parts 4–8: games, STaR, information limits, voting, search with a check, collapse STaR [15]; Mind the Gap [16]; Large Language Monkeys [20]; resampling limits [21]; FOBAR [22]; model collapse [17, 18]; weak-to-strong [19]

A test of understanding. For each key result, the reader should be able to (1) derive it on a blank page, (2) reproduce its worked example without notes, and (3) state in one sentence its implication for a Challenger–Solver loop.
