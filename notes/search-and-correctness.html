18 Search and correctness: finding and recognizing rare answers

Section 17 showed that a vote cannot fix a question whose mode is wrong. A sharper fact makes this worse: when the vote is wrong, the right answer is often not among the samples at all. For GPT-3.5 on math word problems, about \(40\%\) of the failures have no correct chain among the samples (FOBAR, Section 18.6), weaker models do worse, and the correct answers that do appear are often sampled at \(1\%\) or lower (Section 18.3). So the next question has two halves, exactly the two things AlphaGo’s search had and label-free self-play lacks: find the right answer, then recognize it without an answer key.

Key result | one question, two steps

Let \(p\) be the chance that one sample is right, \(k\) the number of samples, and let a check accept a right answer with probability \(\mathrm{TPR}\) and a wrong one with probability \(\mathrm{FPR}\). Then \[\begin{gathered}\Pr[\text{found at least once}]=1-(1-p)^k, \\[4pt] k_{90\%}=\frac{\ln 10}{-\ln(1-p)}\approx\frac{2.3}{p}\end{gathered}\] samples are needed to find it with probability \(0.9\), and an accepted sample is right with probability \[\begin{gathered}\mathrm{prec}=\frac{p\,\mathrm{TPR}}{p\,\mathrm{TPR}+(1-p)\,\mathrm{FPR}} \\[4pt] \text{(Section 16)}.\end{gathered}\]

Derivation. The \(k\) samples all miss with probability \((1-p)^k\); setting \((1-p)^k=0.1\) and taking logs gives \(k_{90\%}\), and \(-\ln(1-p)\approx p\) for small \(p\). The precision is Bayes’ rule, as in Section 16.

Worked example | why 10 samples are not enough

A question the model solves \(5\%\) of the time is found in 10 samples with probability \(1-0.95^{10}=0.40\). Finding it \(90\%\) of the time needs \(\ln10/(-\ln0.95)=45\) samples; at \(p=1\%\) it needs \(230\). A question with \(p=0\) is never found by sampling, at any \(k\): only a different procedure (a hint, an easier stepping stone, a decomposition, new information) can reach it (Section 15).

18.1 The base-rate trap: rare answers need strict checks

Key result | how strict the check must be

Accepted answers are right more often than not, \(\mathrm{prec}\ge\tfrac12\), if and only if \[\mathrm{FPR}\le\frac{p}{1-p}\,\mathrm{TPR}\ \approx\ p\,\mathrm{TPR}.\] The check’s false-accept rate must be smaller than the rate at which the right answer appears.

Derivation. \(\mathrm{prec}\ge\tfrac12\iff p\,\mathrm{TPR}\ge(1-p)\,\mathrm{FPR}\).

Worked example | a good-looking check that fails

The right answer is rare, \(p=0.02\), and the check looks strong: \(\mathrm{TPR}=0.9\), \(\mathrm{FPR}=0.05\). Then \(\mathrm{prec}=0.018/(0.018+0.049)=0.27\): almost three of every four “verified” answers are wrong. In counts, \(100\) samples give \(1.8\) right answers accepted and \(4.9\) wrong ones. With \(\mathrm{FPR}=0.005\) the precision is \(0.018/(0.018+0.0049)=0.79\). A false-accept rate about ten times smaller than the rate of the right answer makes search useful.

18.2 A verified vote and the shared-mistake condition

Accepting any checked sample wastes the vote’s information. A better rule groups the accepted samples by answer and returns the answer with the most accepted samples (the plain vote if nothing is accepted). Let \(\pi_a\) be the share of samples with answer \(a\), let the truth \(t\) pass the check with probability \(\mathrm{TPR}\), and let each wrong answer \(w\) pass with its own rate \(\mathrm{FPR}_w\).

Key result | when a verified vote finds the truth

As \(k\to\infty\) the verified vote returns the truth if and only if \[\pi_t\,\mathrm{TPR}\;>\;\max_{w\ne t}\ \pi_w\,\mathrm{FPR}_w ,\] and for finite \(k\), \(\Pr[w\text{ ties or beats }t]\le\exp\!\big(-\tfrac k2\,(\pi_t\mathrm{TPR}-\pi_w\mathrm{FPR}_w)^2\big)\).

Derivation. Answer \(a\) collects about \(k\,\pi_a\,\Pr[\text{accept}\mid a]\) accepted samples, so the law of large numbers picks the largest \(\pi_a\Pr[\text{accept}\mid a]\). For the finite bound, score a sample \(+1\) if it says \(t\) and is accepted, \(-1\) if it says \(w\) and is accepted, \(0\) otherwise; the mean is \(\pi_t\mathrm{TPR}-\pi_w\mathrm{FPR}_w\), each score lies in an interval of length \(2\), and Hoeffding’s inequality gives the bound, exactly as for plurality voting in Section 17.

Worked example | the check must reject the wrong mode, not wrong answers in general

A shared mistake gives the wrong answer \(w\) half the samples, \(\pi_w=0.5\), and the truth \(\pi_t=0.2\). With \(\mathrm{TPR}=0.9\) the verified vote finds the truth only if \(\mathrm{FPR}_w<0.2\times0.9/0.5=0.36\): the check must reject the shared wrong answer at least \(64\%\) of the time. A check that rereads the problem the same wrong way accepts \(w\) almost always (\(\mathrm{FPR}_w\approx1\)), and the verified vote returns \(w\) exactly like the plain vote. The average false-accept rate over all wrong answers can look excellent while \(\mathrm{FPR}_w\) on the mode is the only number that matters.

Pitfall

Report a check’s \(\mathrm{TPR}\) and \(\mathrm{FPR}\) separately on the vote’s wrong mode (the questions where the vote is wrong), not averaged over all questions. Signals look better where the truth is already the majority, so a probe or confidence score can look strong overall and still fail inside the minority-truth zone, the only place where it could help. Compare it there with the model’s own token probability.

18.3 What more samples buy: the coverage law

Brown et al. (Large Language Monkeys) separate two quantities. Coverage is “the fraction of problems that are solved by any generated sample” (pass@\(k\), Section 7); precision asks “how often can we identify correct samples from our collection of generations?”

Definition | the coverage law (Brown et al., Eqs. 2–3)

\[\begin{gathered}\log C\approx a\,k^{b} \\[4pt] \Longleftrightarrow \\[4pt] C\approx\exp\!\big(a\,k^{b}\big),\end{gathered}\] with \(C\) the coverage, \(k\) the number of samples and \(a,b\) fitted (\(b<0\), so \(C\to1\) as \(k\to\infty\)). Inverting gives the budget for a target coverage: \(k\approx(\ln C/a)^{1/b}\).

Worked example | Llama-3-8B-Instruct on MATH

The paper’s fit is \(a=-1.33\), \(b=-0.43\). It predicts coverage \(0.26\) at \(k=1\), \(0.61\) at \(k=10\) and \(0.83\) at \(k=100\) (the paper measures \(82.9\%\) at \(100\) samples and \(98.44\%\) at \(10{,}000\)). Reaching \(90\%\) coverage needs \(k\approx(\ln0.9/{-1.33})^{-1/0.43}\approx364\) samples.

Key result | finding grows, choosing stalls

On the same model and benchmark, coverage rises from \(82.9\%\) to \(98.44\%\) between \(100\) and \(10{,}000\) samples, while majority voting and reward-model selection rise only from \(40.50\%\) to \(41.41\%\). All selection methods “saturate before reaching 100 samples”, because “the occurrence of rare, correct solutions does not affect the most common answer that majority voting chooses”; those correct solutions are sampled “at low probabilities (e.g. 1% or lower)”.

With an automatic verifier, coverage becomes real accuracy: on SWE-bench Lite, DeepSeek-Coder-V2 rises from \(15.9\%\) with one sample to \(56\%\) with \(250\), above the single-sample state of the art (\(43\%\)). And rare correct answers are mostly real: over \(90\%\) of the graded chains of thought were faithful, even where correct answers were rare. The needle is in the haystack; what is missing is a way to recognize it.

18.4 Why an imperfect check caps search

Stroebl, Kapoor and Narayanan (The Limits of Inference Scaling Through Resampling) study code with unit tests that sometimes pass wrong programs. They name the two error rates: completeness, the “probability of accepting a correct solution” (our \(\mathrm{TPR}\)), and soundness, the “probability of rejecting an incorrect solution” (our \(1-\mathrm{FPR}\)). Below, \(p\) is again the chance that one sample is correct (the paper calls it \(r\)).

Key result | the resampling ceiling

Resample until the check accepts. A false positive returned at any attempt is final, and “resampling cannot decrease this probability, so it imposes an upper bound to the accuracy of resampling-based inference scaling, regardless of compute budget”. On one task type, the accuracy of the returned answer is the precision of Section 16, \[\mathrm{prec}=\frac{p\,\mathrm{TPR}}{p\,\mathrm{TPR}+(1-p)\,\mathrm{FPR}},\] whatever the budget \(K\). Hence: if \(P_{\text{strong}}(\text{correct})>P_{\text{weak}}(\text{correct}\mid\text{pass})\), “the weaker model cannot match the performance of a single invocation of the stronger model, no matter how big the compute budget”.

Derivation. Each attempt ends the search with a correct answer with probability \(p\,\mathrm{TPR}\) and with a wrong one with probability \((1-p)\,\mathrm{FPR}\); otherwise it continues. Whenever the search ends, the answer is correct with probability \(p\,\mathrm{TPR}/(p\,\mathrm{TPR}+(1-p)\,\mathrm{FPR})\), so the accuracy does not depend on \(K\); more attempts only raise the chance that the search ends at all.

Worked example | easy and hard tasks

The paper’s two-type model for Llama-3.1-8B on HumanEval+ has easy tasks with \(p=0.87\), hard tasks with \(p=0.13\), \(\mathrm{TPR}=1\) and \(\mathrm{FPR}=0.25\). On hard tasks the precision is \(0.13/(0.13+0.25\times0.87)=0.37\): most accepted answers are wrong. Each rejection makes a hard task more likely: after \(k-1\) rejections, \(\Pr[\text{type }i]\propto\beta_i^{\,k-1}w_i\), where \(w_i\) is the share of tasks of type \(i\) and \(\beta_i=(1-\mathrm{TPR})\,p_i+(1-\mathrm{FPR})(1-p_i)\) is its rejection rate. So the false-positive share grows with the number of attempts: “the easy tasks get solved within a few attempts, and for the remaining harder tasks, false positives are more likely”. Measured: Llama-3.1-70B’s false-positive share rises from about \(0.13\) at \(K=1\) to about \(0.65\) at large \(K\), and when a false positive costs four times what a true one gains, the best budget is \(K\le5\) for all four models tested.

Pitfall

Their model treats rejections as independent draws. A self-check whose errors follow the model’s own misreading is worse: it accepts the same wrong answer again and again. That is the case \(\mathrm{FPR}_w\approx1\) of the verified-vote condition, and no number of samples escapes it.

18.5 Where a strict check can come from

By the data processing inequality (Section 15), a check computed from the model’s own outputs adds no new information. A check helps only through an asymmetry: verifying is easier than solving, or the check reads something the mistake does not control (Section 17.8). Four practical sources:

Source Why it is easier than solving Where it breaks
Plug the answer back in substituting \(7\) into \(3x+5=26\) is trivial only for answers that can be substituted
Backward check (FOBAR) recovering a masked number from the answer reads the problem in another direction if the misreading is reused, the check confirms it
Answer-first construction the answer is fixed before the question is written the written text may not match the intended problem
Execution (Absolute Zero) a program’s output is computed, not judged only for executable tasks (code)

18.6 The backward check in practice: FOBAR

FOBAR (Jiang et al.) checks a candidate answer by turning the question around. Every number in the question is replaced, one at a time, by \(x\), and the model is asked:

“If we know the answer to the above question is \(\{\hat A_j\}\), what is the value of unknown variable \(x\)?”

Definition | forward–backward scoring (FOBAR, Eqs. 1–4)

For candidate answers \(\hat A_j\) (the distinct forward answers, set \(\mathcal A\)), forward: \(\mathbb P_F(\hat A_j)=\frac1{M_F}\sum_{i=1}^{M_F}\mathbf{1}(A_i=\hat A_j)\), the vote share. Backward: with \(N_Q\) masked numbers and \(M_B\) backward samples each, \[\begin{gathered}Z_j=\sum_{n=1}^{N_Q}\sum_{b=1}^{M_B}\mathbf{1}\big(\widehat{\mathrm{num}}^{(n)}_{j,b}=\mathrm{num}^{(n)}\big), \\[4pt] \mathbb P_B(\hat A_j)=\frac{Z_j+\epsilon}{\sum_{j'}Z_{j'}+\epsilon|\mathcal A|},\end{gathered}\] and the final answer maximizes \(\mathbb P(\hat A_j)\propto\mathbb P_F(\hat A_j)^{\alpha}\,\mathbb P_B(\hat A_j)^{1-\alpha}\), with \(\alpha=0.5\) (\(\alpha=1\) is plain self-consistency). We write \(j\) for the paper’s candidate index \(c\). The paper uses \(M_B=8\), temperature \(0.7\), and exact string match.

Connection. With \(\alpha=\tfrac12\) the argmax of \(\sqrt{\mathbb P_F\,\mathbb P_B}\) is the argmax of \(\mathbb P_F\,\mathbb P_B\propto\pi_a\,\Pr[\text{check passes}\mid a]\): FOBAR is exactly the verified vote above, so its success condition is \(\pi_t\,\mathrm{TPR}>\pi_w\,\mathrm{FPR}_w\).

Worked example | what the paper’s numbers say

  • The check is weak. A masked number is recovered \(41\)–\(53\%\) of the time from the correct candidate, but also \(9\)–\(25\%\) of the time from a wrong one (GPT-4: \(25.0\%\) and \(19.2\%\); Fig. 5). So \(\mathrm{TPR}\approx0.5\) and \(\mathrm{FPR}\approx0.1\)–\(0.25\) per backward sample.

  • Gains shrink as models get stronger. Average over six datasets, self-consistency \(\to\) FOBAR: \(81.4\to83.5\) (text-davinci-003), \(85.5\to86.6\) (GPT-3.5), \(91.1\to91.4\) (GPT-4) (Table 1, CoT prompts).

  • The absent-answer ceiling is in the paper too. For GPT-3.5, “about \(60\%\) of the total failure cases have at least one correct chain (the remaining \(40\%\) have no correct chains and thus cannot be solved by backward reasoning)”; FOBAR fixed \(54\) of those \(294\) (Sec. 4.8).

  • A shared misreading can pass the check. In one example the gold answer is \(1.9\) dollars, yet the backward score prefers \(190\) (cents), \(0.57\) to \(0.43\) (App. C.2).

Plug the paper’s rates into the verified-vote condition: with \(\mathrm{TPR}=0.5\) and \(\mathrm{FPR}_w=0.2\), the truth wins only if \(\pi_t>0.4\,\pi_w\); against a wrong mode with half the samples, the truth needs at least \(20\%\) of them. The paper’s own limitations: masking a redundant number makes it unpredictable, and choosing what to mask is hard outside math.

18.7 Self-play with a perfect check: Absolute Zero

Absolute Zero (Zhao et al.) is the cleanest AlphaGo-like loop for language models: one model proposes and solves tasks, and a Python executor is the referee. A task is a triplet \((p,i,o)\) with \(o=p(i)\):

Task Given \(\to\) predict How the executor checks
Deduction \((p,i)\to o\) value equality of the predicted and true output
Abduction \((p,o)\to i\) \(p(i_\pi)=o\); any input that gives the output counts, because programs need not be one-to-one
Induction half of the \((i,o)\) pairs and a message \(\to p\) \(p_\pi\) must reproduce held-out pairs, which blocks if-else memorization
Definition | rewards and advantages (Absolute Zero, Eqs. 4–8)

Proposer: \(r_{\mathrm{propose}}=0\) if \(\bar r_{\mathrm{solve}}=0\), else \(1-\bar r_{\mathrm{solve}}\), with \(\bar r_{\mathrm{solve}}\) the mean of \(G=8\) solver attempts. Solver: \(r_{\mathrm{solve}}=\mathbf{1}(y=y^\star)\) by value equality in Python. Format: \(-0.5\) for a well-formatted wrong answer, \(-1\) for a format error. Advantages use six separate baselines, one per (task, role): \[\begin{gathered}A^{\mathrm{norm}}_{\mathrm{task,role}}=\frac{r-\mu_{\mathrm{task,role}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{task,role}}}, \\[4pt] \mathrm{task}\in\{\mathrm{ind},\mathrm{ded},\mathrm{abd}\},\ \mathrm{role}\in\{\mathrm{propose},\mathrm{solve}\}.\end{gathered}\] Proposed tasks are kept only if the program runs, uses no banned packages, and gives the same output twice (determinism check).

Worked example | what transfers, and what does not

AZR-Coder-7B raises the average over code and math benchmarks from \(40.2\) to \(50.4\), with math \(+15.2\) points, and beats earlier zero-data models by \(1.8\) points on average (Table 1). But its math score (\(39.1\)) stays below zero-RL models trained on curated math (for example \(45.8\)): the math gain is transfer from checked code, not checked math. Gains grow with size (\(+5.7\), \(+10.2\), \(+13.2\) for 3B, 7B, 14B), and the authors report that composite programs collapsed to the trivial \(f(g(x))=g(x)\), a proposer gaming the format of its tasks. The lesson: with an executor, rare successes are recognized with \(\mathrm{FPR}\approx0\); natural-language math has none, so the question is how close a cheap check can get to \(\mathrm{FPR}_w\approx0\) on the shared wrong answer.

Why it matters for self-play

Together, these papers give one recipe for testing a new check. (1) Find: on the questions where the vote is wrong, measure how often the truth appears among the samples, and how that grows with \(k\) (the coverage law). (2) Recognize: measure the check’s \(\mathrm{TPR}\) on the truth and its \(\mathrm{FPR}_w\) on the vote’s wrong answer, not on average, and test \(\pi_t\,\mathrm{TPR}>\pi_w\,\mathrm{FPR}_w\) question by question. (3) Same compute: a check that costs as much as ten extra samples must beat a vote with ten extra samples. (4) The ceiling: if the check accepts the shared wrong answer about as often as the truth appears, more search makes the wrong answer more certain, not less.

Exercises

Exercise 15. A question is solved by \(3\%\) of samples. How many samples give a \(90\%\) chance of finding the right answer at least once?

Show answer

\(k=\ln10/(-\ln0.97)=2.303/0.0305=75.6\), so \(76\) samples (the rule of thumb \(2.3/p\) gives \(77\)).

Exercise 16. A check has \(\mathrm{TPR}=0.8\) and \(\mathrm{FPR}=0.1\), and the right answer appears in \(5\%\) of samples. What share of accepted answers is right? What is the largest \(\mathrm{FPR}\) that keeps it at or above one half?

Show answer

\(\mathrm{prec}=0.04/(0.04+0.095)=0.30\). It reaches one half when \(\mathrm{FPR}\le\frac{p}{1-p}\mathrm{TPR}=\frac{0.05}{0.95}\times0.8=0.042\).

Exercise 17. The truth has \(\pi_t=0.3\) of the samples and the vote’s wrong answer has \(\pi_w=0.4\). The check accepts the truth with \(\mathrm{TPR}=0.9\). Below what \(\mathrm{FPR}_w\) on the wrong answer does a verified vote pick the truth for large \(k\)?

Show answer

\(\mathrm{FPR}_w<\pi_t\,\mathrm{TPR}/\pi_w=0.27/0.4=0.675\): the check must reject the wrong answer at least about a third of the time.
