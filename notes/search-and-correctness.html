Search and Correctness: Finding and Recognizing Rare Answers

Date: November 6, 2026  |  Estimated Reading Time: 21 min  |  Author: Chandak Chakma

Finding a rare correct answer requires many samples; recognizing it requires a check whose false-positive rate lies below the answer’s own frequency. We cover coverage laws, the resampling ceiling, a verified-vote condition, FOBAR and Absolute Zero.

Part 7 of 10 in Foundations of Self-Play for LLM Reasoning. The complete series is also available as a single PDF.

Section 17 showed that majority voting cannot correct a question whose mode is wrong. A stronger observation compounds the problem: when the vote is wrong, the correct answer is often absent from the samples altogether. For GPT-3.5 on math word problems, about \(40\%\) of failures contain no correct chain among the samples (FOBAR, Section 18.6); weaker models fare worse, and the correct answers that do appear are often sampled with probability \(1\%\) or lower (Section 18.3). The next question therefore has two parts, corresponding precisely to the two capabilities that AlphaGo’s search possessed and that label-free self-play lacks: we must find the correct answer and then recognize it without an answer key.

Key resultone question, two steps

Let \(p\) denote the probability that a single sample is correct and \(k\) the number of samples, and suppose a check accepts a correct answer with probability \(\mathrm{TPR}\) and an incorrect one with probability \(\mathrm{FPR}\). Then \[\begin{gathered}\Pr[\text{found at least once}]=1-(1-p)^k, \\[4pt] k_{90\%}=\frac{\ln 10}{-\ln(1-p)}\approx\frac{2.3}{p}\end{gathered}\] where the second expression is the number of samples needed to find the correct answer with probability \(0.9\). An accepted sample is correct with probability \[\begin{gathered}\mathrm{prec}=\frac{p\,\mathrm{TPR}}{p\,\mathrm{TPR}+(1-p)\,\mathrm{FPR}} \\[4pt] \text{(Section 16)}.\end{gathered}\]

Derivation. All \(k\) samples miss with probability \((1-p)^k\); setting \((1-p)^k=0.1\) and taking logarithms yields \(k_{90\%}\), and the approximation follows from \(-\ln(1-p)\approx p\) for small \(p\). The precision follows from Bayes’ rule, as in Section 16.

Examplewhy 10 samples do not suffice

For a question the model solves \(5\%\) of the time, 10 samples contain the correct answer with probability \(1-0.95^{10}=0.40\). Finding it with probability \(90\%\) requires \(\ln10/(-\ln0.95)=45\) samples, and at \(p=1\%\) the requirement rises to \(230\). A question with \(p=0\) is never found by sampling, for any \(k\); only a different procedure (a hint, an easier stepping-stone problem, a decomposition, or new information) can reach it (Section 15).

18.1 The base-rate trap: rare answers require strict checks

Key resulthow strict the check must be

Accepted answers are correct at least as often as not, \(\mathrm{prec}\ge\tfrac12\), if and only if \[\mathrm{FPR}\le\frac{p}{1-p}\,\mathrm{TPR}\ \approx\ p\,\mathrm{TPR}.\] That is, the check’s false-positive rate must be smaller than the rate at which the correct answer appears.

Derivation. Rearranging the definition of precision gives \(\mathrm{prec}\ge\tfrac12\iff p\,\mathrm{TPR}\ge(1-p)\,\mathrm{FPR}\).

Examplea seemingly strong check that fails

Suppose the correct answer is rare, \(p=0.02\), and the check appears strong, with \(\mathrm{TPR}=0.9\) and \(\mathrm{FPR}=0.05\). Then \(\mathrm{prec}=0.018/(0.018+0.049)=0.27\): nearly three of every four “verified” answers are wrong. In terms of counts, out of \(100\) samples, \(1.8\) correct answers and \(4.9\) incorrect ones are accepted. With \(\mathrm{FPR}=0.005\), the precision rises to \(0.018/(0.018+0.0049)=0.79\). A false-positive rate roughly ten times smaller than the rate of the correct answer is what makes search worthwhile.

18.2 The verified vote and the shared-error condition

Returning any sample that passes the check discards the information contained in the vote. A better rule, the verified vote, groups the accepted samples by answer and returns the answer with the most accepted samples, falling back to the plain majority vote if no sample is accepted. Let \(\pi_a\) denote the fraction of samples with answer \(a\), let the true answer \(t\) pass the check with probability \(\mathrm{TPR}\), and let each wrong answer \(w\) pass with its own rate \(\mathrm{FPR}_w\).

Key resultwhen a verified vote finds the truth

As \(k\to\infty\), the verified vote returns the true answer if and only if \[\pi_t\,\mathrm{TPR}\;>\;\max_{w\ne t}\ \pi_w\,\mathrm{FPR}_w ,\] and, for finite \(k\), \(\Pr[w\text{ ties or beats }t]\le\exp\!\big(-\tfrac k2\,(\pi_t\mathrm{TPR}-\pi_w\mathrm{FPR}_w)^2\big)\).

Derivation. Answer \(a\) collects approximately \(k\,\pi_a\,\Pr[\text{accept}\mid a]\) accepted samples, so by the law of large numbers the verified vote selects the answer with the largest \(\pi_a\Pr[\text{accept}\mid a]\). For the finite-sample bound, we assign each sample a score of \(+1\) if it answers \(t\) and is accepted, \(-1\) if it answers \(w\) and is accepted, and \(0\) otherwise. The mean score is \(\pi_t\mathrm{TPR}-\pi_w\mathrm{FPR}_w\), each score lies in an interval of length \(2\), and Hoeffding’s inequality yields the bound, exactly as for plurality voting in Section 17.

Examplethe check must reject the wrong mode, not wrong answers in general

Suppose a shared error gives the wrong answer \(w\) half of the samples, \(\pi_w=0.5\), while the truth receives \(\pi_t=0.2\). With \(\mathrm{TPR}=0.9\), the verified vote recovers the truth only if \(\mathrm{FPR}_w<0.2\times0.9/0.5=0.36\); that is, the check must reject the shared wrong answer at least \(64\%\) of the time. A check that rereads the problem with the same misinterpretation accepts \(w\) almost always (\(\mathrm{FPR}_w\approx1\)), and the verified vote then returns \(w\), exactly as the plain majority vote does. The false-positive rate averaged over all wrong answers can look excellent even though \(\mathrm{FPR}_w\) on the mode is the only quantity that matters.

Pitfall

A check’s \(\mathrm{TPR}\) and \(\mathrm{FPR}\) should be reported separately on the vote’s wrong mode (the questions on which the vote is wrong), not averaged over all questions. Signals tend to look better where the truth already holds the majority, so a probe or confidence score can appear strong overall yet fail within the minority-truth regime, which is the only place where it could help. Within that regime, it should be compared against the model’s own token probability.

18.3 What additional samples buy: the coverage law

Brown et al. (Large Language Monkeys) distinguish two quantities. Coverage is “the fraction of problems that are solved by any generated sample” (pass@\(k\), Section 7), whereas precision asks “how often can we identify correct samples from our collection of generations?”

Definitionthe coverage law (Brown et al., Eqs. 2–3)

\[\begin{gathered}\log C\approx a\,k^{b} \\[4pt] \Longleftrightarrow \\[4pt] C\approx\exp\!\big(a\,k^{b}\big),\end{gathered}\] where \(C\) is the coverage, \(k\) the number of samples, and \(a,b\) are fitted parameters (\(b<0\), so \(C\to1\) as \(k\to\infty\)). Inverting the law gives the sample budget required for a target coverage: \(k\approx(\ln C/a)^{1/b}\).

ExampleLlama-3-8B-Instruct on MATH

The paper’s fitted parameters are \(a=-1.33\) and \(b=-0.43\). The fit predicts a coverage of \(0.26\) at \(k=1\), \(0.61\) at \(k=10\), and \(0.83\) at \(k=100\) (the paper reports measured values of \(82.9\%\) at \(100\) samples and \(98.44\%\) at \(10{,}000\)). Reaching \(90\%\) coverage requires \(k\approx(\ln0.9/{-1.33})^{-1/0.43}\approx364\) samples.

Key resultfinding scales, selection saturates

On the same model and benchmark, coverage rises from \(82.9\%\) to \(98.44\%\) as the number of samples grows from \(100\) to \(10{,}000\), whereas majority voting and reward-model selection improve only from \(40.50\%\) to \(41.41\%\). All selection methods “saturate before reaching 100 samples”, because “the occurrence of rare, correct solutions does not affect the most common answer that majority voting chooses”; such correct solutions are sampled “at low probabilities (e.g. 1% or lower)”.

With an automatic verifier, coverage translates directly into accuracy: on SWE-bench Lite, DeepSeek-Coder-V2 improves from \(15.9\%\) with one sample to \(56\%\) with \(250\), exceeding the single-sample state of the art (\(43\%\)). Moreover, rare correct answers are mostly genuine: over \(90\%\) of the graded chains of thought were faithful, even where correct answers were rare. In these settings the correct answer is present among the samples; what is missing is a reliable means of recognizing it.

18.4 Why an imperfect check caps search

Stroebl, Kapoor and Narayanan (The Limits of Inference Scaling Through Resampling) study code generation with unit tests that occasionally pass incorrect programs. They name the two error rates completeness, the “probability of accepting a correct solution” (our \(\mathrm{TPR}\)), and soundness, the “probability of rejecting an incorrect solution” (our \(1-\mathrm{FPR}\)). As before, \(p\) denotes the probability that a single sample is correct (the paper denotes it \(r\)).

Key resultthe resampling ceiling

Consider resampling until the check accepts. A false positive returned at any attempt is final, and “resampling cannot decrease this probability, so it imposes an upper bound to the accuracy of resampling-based inference scaling, regardless of compute budget”. For a single task type, the accuracy of the returned answer equals the precision of Section 16, \[\mathrm{prec}=\frac{p\,\mathrm{TPR}}{p\,\mathrm{TPR}+(1-p)\,\mathrm{FPR}},\] regardless of the budget \(K\). Consequently, if \(P_{\text{strong}}(\text{correct})>P_{\text{weak}}(\text{correct}\mid\text{pass})\), then “the weaker model cannot match the performance of a single invocation of the stronger model, no matter how big the compute budget”.

Derivation. Each attempt terminates the search with a correct answer with probability \(p\,\mathrm{TPR}\) and with an incorrect one with probability \((1-p)\,\mathrm{FPR}\); otherwise the search continues. Whenever the search terminates, the answer is correct with probability \(p\,\mathrm{TPR}/(p\,\mathrm{TPR}+(1-p)\,\mathrm{FPR})\), so the accuracy is independent of \(K\); additional attempts only increase the probability that the search terminates at all.

Exampleeasy and hard tasks

The paper’s two-type model for Llama-3.1-8B on HumanEval+ has easy tasks with \(p=0.87\) and hard tasks with \(p=0.13\), with \(\mathrm{TPR}=1\) and \(\mathrm{FPR}=0.25\). On hard tasks, the precision is \(0.13/(0.13+0.25\times0.87)=0.37\), so most accepted answers are wrong. Each rejection raises the posterior probability that the task is hard: after \(k-1\) rejections, \(\Pr[\text{type }i]\propto\beta_i^{\,k-1}w_i\), where \(w_i\) is the fraction of tasks of type \(i\) and \(\beta_i=(1-\mathrm{TPR})\,p_i+(1-\mathrm{FPR})(1-p_i)\) is its rejection rate. Consequently, the share of false positives grows with the number of attempts: “the easy tasks get solved within a few attempts, and for the remaining harder tasks, false positives are more likely”. Empirically, the false-positive share of Llama-3.1-70B rises from about \(0.13\) at \(K=1\) to about \(0.65\) at large \(K\), and when a false positive costs four times as much as a true positive gains, the optimal budget is \(K\le5\) for all four models tested.

Pitfall

The analysis of Stroebl et al. treats rejections as independent draws. A self-check whose errors track the model’s own misreading behaves worse, because it accepts the same wrong answer repeatedly. This is the case \(\mathrm{FPR}_w\approx1\) of the verified-vote condition, from which no number of samples can escape.

18.5 Sources of strict checks

By the data processing inequality (Section 15), a check computed solely from the model’s own outputs adds no new information. A check can help only by exploiting an asymmetry: either verification is easier than solving, or the check depends on something the error does not control (Section 17.8). We list four practical sources:

Source Why it is easier than solving Where it fails
Substitution substituting \(7\) into \(3x+5=26\) is trivial applies only to answers that can be substituted
Backward check (FOBAR) recovering a masked number from the answer reads the problem in the reverse direction if the same misreading is reused, the check confirms it
Answer-first construction the answer is fixed before the question is written the written question may not match the intended problem
Execution (Absolute Zero) a program’s output is computed rather than judged applies only to executable tasks (code)

18.6 The backward check in practice: FOBAR

FOBAR (Jiang et al.) verifies a candidate answer by inverting the question. Each number in the question is replaced in turn by \(x\), and the model is asked:

“If we know the answer to the above question is \(\{\hat A_j\}\), what is the value of unknown variable \(x\)?”

Definitionforward–backward scoring (FOBAR, Eqs. 1–4)

Let the candidate answers \(\hat A_j\) be the distinct forward answers, forming the set \(\mathcal A\). The forward score is the vote share, \(\mathbb P_F(\hat A_j)=\frac1{M_F}\sum_{i=1}^{M_F}\mathbf{1}(A_i=\hat A_j)\). For the backward score, with \(N_Q\) masked numbers and \(M_B\) backward samples per number, \[\begin{gathered}Z_j=\sum_{n=1}^{N_Q}\sum_{b=1}^{M_B}\mathbf{1}\big(\widehat{\mathrm{num}}^{(n)}_{j,b}=\mathrm{num}^{(n)}\big), \\[4pt] \mathbb P_B(\hat A_j)=\frac{Z_j+\epsilon}{\sum_{j'}Z_{j'}+\epsilon|\mathcal A|},\end{gathered}\] and the final answer maximizes \(\mathbb P(\hat A_j)\propto\mathbb P_F(\hat A_j)^{\alpha}\,\mathbb P_B(\hat A_j)^{1-\alpha}\), with \(\alpha=0.5\) (\(\alpha=1\) recovers plain self-consistency). We write \(j\) for the paper’s candidate index \(c\). The paper uses \(M_B=8\), temperature \(0.7\), and exact string matching.

Connection. With \(\alpha=\tfrac12\), the maximizer of \(\sqrt{\mathbb P_F\,\mathbb P_B}\) coincides with the maximizer of \(\mathbb P_F\,\mathbb P_B\propto\pi_a\,\Pr[\text{check passes}\mid a]\). FOBAR is therefore exactly the verified vote defined above, and its success condition is \(\pi_t\,\mathrm{TPR}>\pi_w\,\mathrm{FPR}_w\).

Examplewhat the paper’s numbers show

  • The check is weak. A masked number is recovered \(41\)–\(53\%\) of the time from the correct candidate, but also \(9\)–\(25\%\) of the time from an incorrect one (GPT-4: \(25.0\%\) and \(19.2\%\); Fig. 5). Hence \(\mathrm{TPR}\approx0.5\) and \(\mathrm{FPR}\approx0.1\)–\(0.25\) per backward sample.

  • Gains shrink as models become stronger. Average accuracy over six datasets, self-consistency \(\to\) FOBAR, is \(81.4\to83.5\) (text-davinci-003), \(85.5\to86.6\) (GPT-3.5), and \(91.1\to91.4\) (GPT-4) (Table 1, CoT prompts).

  • The paper also exhibits the absent-answer ceiling. For GPT-3.5, “about \(60\%\) of the total failure cases have at least one correct chain (the remaining \(40\%\) have no correct chains and thus cannot be solved by backward reasoning)”; FOBAR corrected \(54\) of those \(294\) cases (Sec. 4.8).

  • A shared misreading can pass the check. In one example, the gold answer is \(1.9\) dollars, yet the backward score prefers \(190\) (cents) by \(0.57\) to \(0.43\) (App. C.2).

Substituting the paper’s rates into the verified-vote condition, with \(\mathrm{TPR}=0.5\) and \(\mathrm{FPR}_w=0.2\), the truth wins only if \(\pi_t>0.4\,\pi_w\); against a wrong mode holding half of the samples, the truth needs at least \(20\%\) of them. The paper itself notes two limitations: masking a redundant number renders it unpredictable, and choosing what to mask is difficult outside mathematics.

18.7 Self-play with a perfect check: Absolute Zero

Absolute Zero (Zhao et al.) is the cleanest AlphaGo-like loop for language models: a single model both proposes and solves tasks, and a Python executor serves as the referee. Each task is a triplet \((p,i,o)\) with \(o=p(i)\), and three task types are defined:

Task Given \(\to\) predict How the executor checks
Deduction \((p,i)\to o\) value equality between the predicted and true outputs
Abduction \((p,o)\to i\) \(p(i_\pi)=o\); any input that produces the output is accepted, since programs need not be one-to-one
Induction half of the \((i,o)\) pairs and a message \(\to p\) \(p_\pi\) must reproduce held-out pairs, which prevents memorization through if-else branches
Definitionrewards and advantages (Absolute Zero, Eqs. 4–8)

Proposer: \(r_{\mathrm{propose}}=0\) if \(\bar r_{\mathrm{solve}}=0\), and \(1-\bar r_{\mathrm{solve}}\) otherwise, where \(\bar r_{\mathrm{solve}}\) is the mean over \(G=8\) solver attempts. Solver: \(r_{\mathrm{solve}}=\mathbf{1}(y=y^\star)\), evaluated by value equality in Python. Format penalties: \(-0.5\) for a well-formatted wrong answer and \(-1\) for a format error. Advantages are normalized with six separate baselines, one per (task, role) pair: \[\begin{gathered}A^{\mathrm{norm}}_{\mathrm{task,role}}=\frac{r-\mu_{\mathrm{task,role}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{task,role}}}, \\[4pt] \mathrm{task}\in\{\mathrm{ind},\mathrm{ded},\mathrm{abd}\},\ \mathrm{role}\in\{\mathrm{propose},\mathrm{solve}\}.\end{gathered}\] A proposed task is retained only if the program runs, uses no banned packages, and produces the same output on two executions (a determinism check).

Examplewhat transfers, and what does not

AZR-Coder-7B raises the average over code and math benchmarks from \(40.2\) to \(50.4\), including a gain of \(+15.2\) points in math, and outperforms earlier zero-data models by \(1.8\) points on average (Table 1). Its math score (\(39.1\)) nevertheless remains below that of zero-RL models trained on curated math data (for example, \(45.8\)): the math gain is transfer from verified code, not from verified math. Gains increase with model size (\(+5.7\), \(+10.2\), \(+13.2\) for 3B, 7B, 14B), and the authors report that composite programs collapsed to the trivial form \(f(g(x))=g(x)\), an instance of the proposer gaming the format of its own tasks. The lesson is that, with an executor, rare successes are recognized with \(\mathrm{FPR}\approx0\). Natural-language math has no such executor, so the open question is how closely an inexpensive check can approach \(\mathrm{FPR}_w\approx0\) on the shared wrong answer.

Why it matters

Taken together, these papers yield a single protocol for evaluating a new check. (1) Find: on the questions where the vote is wrong, we measure how often the truth appears among the samples and how this frequency grows with \(k\) (the coverage law). (2) Recognize: we measure the check’s \(\mathrm{TPR}\) on the truth and its \(\mathrm{FPR}_w\) on the vote’s wrong answer, rather than on average, and test \(\pi_t\,\mathrm{TPR}>\pi_w\,\mathrm{FPR}_w\) question by question. (3) Matched compute: a check that costs as much as ten extra samples must outperform a vote with ten extra samples. (4) The ceiling: if the check accepts the shared wrong answer about as often as the truth appears, further search makes the wrong answer more certain, not less.

Exercises

Exercise 15. A question is solved by \(3\%\) of samples. How many samples are needed for a \(90\%\) chance of finding the correct answer at least once?

Show solution

\(k=\ln10/(-\ln0.97)=2.303/0.0305=75.6\), so \(76\) samples are required (the rule of thumb \(2.3/p\) gives \(77\)).

Exercise 16. A check has \(\mathrm{TPR}=0.8\) and \(\mathrm{FPR}=0.1\), and the correct answer appears in \(5\%\) of samples. What fraction of accepted answers is correct? What is the largest \(\mathrm{FPR}\) that keeps this fraction at or above one half?

Show solution

\(\mathrm{prec}=0.04/(0.04+0.095)=0.30\). Precision reaches one half when \(\mathrm{FPR}\le\frac{p}{1-p}\mathrm{TPR}=\frac{0.05}{0.95}\times0.8=0.042\).

Exercise 17. The truth receives \(\pi_t=0.3\) of the samples and the vote’s wrong answer receives \(\pi_w=0.4\). The check accepts the truth with \(\mathrm{TPR}=0.9\). Below what value of \(\mathrm{FPR}_w\) on the wrong answer does the verified vote select the truth for large \(k\)?

Show solution

\(\mathrm{FPR}_w<\pi_t\,\mathrm{TPR}/\pi_w=0.27/0.4=0.675\); that is, the check must reject the incorrect answer in at least roughly one third of cases.
