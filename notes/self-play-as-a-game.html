Self-Play as a Game: Cycling, Expert Iteration and STaR

Simultaneous gradient play can spiral or cycle; fictitious play, opponent pools and PSRO restore convergence. Expert iteration explains AlphaZero, and STaR turns out to be an EM procedure for the log-likelihood of the correct answer.

4.1 Games, equilibria, and cycling in self-play#

DefinitionZero-sum games and equilibria

In a zero-sum game, player 1 receives payoff \(u(a,b)\) and player 2 receives \(-u(a,b)\). For mixed strategies \(s,t\) and payoff matrix \(U\), von Neumann’s minimax theorem states that \(\max_s\min_t s^\top U t=\min_t\max_s s^\top U t\). A Nash equilibrium is a pair of strategies from which neither player can improve its payoff by deviating unilaterally.

Key resultNaive self-play can diverge

Consider \(f(u,v)=uv\), where \(u\) is the minimizing player and \(v\) the maximizing player. Simultaneous gradient steps \(u_{t+1}=u_t-\eta v_t\), \(v_{t+1}=v_t+\eta u_t\) yield \[u_{t+1}^2+v_{t+1}^2=(1+\eta^2)\,(u_t^2+v_t^2),\] so the iterates spiral outward from the equilibrium \((0,0)\) for every nonzero step size.

In rock–paper–scissors, best responses cycle indefinitely: rock is answered by paper, paper by scissors, and scissors by rock. Standard remedies include the following.

  • Fictitious play: each player best-responds to the average of all past opponent strategies; in two-player zero-sum games, the time-averaged strategies converge (Robinson, 1951).

  • Opponent pools: the agent plays against a randomly selected earlier checkpoint, as in AlphaGo.

  • PSRO (Lanctot et al., 2017): a growing population of policies is maintained and mixed according to a Nash equilibrium of the meta-game.

  • Optimistic or extragradient updates: these methods converge on bilinear games, on which plain gradient steps spiral outward.

Why it matters

Challenger–Solver self-play is general-sum and non-stationary: each player’s objective shifts as the other learns. The forgetting of earlier skills, which R-Diverse reports as cross-round mode cycling, is the LLM counterpart of this cycling. Memory banks and policy populations are the LLM analogue of fictitious play.

4.2 Search as a teacher: expert iteration and AlphaZero#

Expert iteration (Anthony et al., 2017) alternates between two steps. First, an improvement operator \(\mathcal I\), such as tree search, filtering, or voting, transforms the current policy into a stronger one; second, the policy is trained to imitate the improved policy, \(\pi\leftarrow\arg\min_\pi D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathcal I(\pi)\,\|\,\pi)\). AlphaZero is the best-known instance, with the loss \[\ell=(z-v)^2-\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathrm{MCTS}}^{\top}\log\mathbf p+c\,\|\theta\|^2 ,\] where \(z\) is the actual game outcome, \(v\) the predicted value, \(\boldsymbol\pi_{\mathrm{MCTS}}\) the search’s move distribution, and \(\mathbf p\) the network’s.

Key resultThe condition for self-improvement

The loop yields improvement only if \(\mathcal I(\pi)\) is reliably better than \(\pi\). In Go, search combined with the true game outcome ensures this. In label-free LLM self-play, the operator is typically a majority vote, which improves on the policy only when the mode is correct.

4.3 STaR as maximum marginal likelihood#

Key resultFisher’s identity

For a latent rationale \(z\) and gold answer \(y^\star\), \[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(y^\star\mid x)=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_\theta(z\mid x,y^\star)}\Big[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(z,y^\star\mid x)\Big].\]

Derivation. \(\nabla_\theta\log p(y^\star\mid x)=\frac{1}{p(y^\star\mid x)}\sum_z\nabla_\theta p(z,y^\star\mid x)=\sum_z\frac{p(z,y^\star\mid x)}{p(y^\star\mid x)}\nabla_\theta\log p(z,y^\star\mid x)\), where the weight is precisely the posterior \(p(z\mid x,y^\star)\). If the answer is a deterministic function of the rationale, \(y=\mathrm{ans}(z)\), then sampling \(z\sim p_\theta(z\mid x)\) and retaining it only when \(\mathrm{ans}(z)=y^\star\) yields exact samples from the posterior. Fine-tuning on the retained rationales, as in STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022), therefore follows a stochastic gradient of \(\log p_\theta(y^\star\mid x)\); the procedure is an EM-style algorithm.

Key resultRL versus likelihood

RL with a \(0/1\) reward maximizes \(p_\theta(y^\star\mid x)\), with \(\nabla_\theta p=p\,\nabla_\theta\log p\), whereas STaR maximizes \(\log p_\theta(y^\star\mid x)\), with \(\nabla_\theta\log p=\nabla_\theta p/p\). On a problem solved \(1\%\) of the time, the likelihood objective therefore weights the gradient \(100\) times more heavily than RL does; log-likelihood thus emphasizes hard problems.

Why it matters

The choice between maximizing \(p\) and maximizing \(\log p\) determines whether rare successes on hard problems drive learning. It is therefore directly connected to the Challenger’s role of keeping \(p\) within the learnable range.

Exercises#

Exercise 1. In the bilinear example with \(\eta=0.1\) and initial point \((u,v)=(1,0)\), what is the distance of the iterates from the equilibrium after \(10\) steps?

Show solution

The squared radius grows by a factor of \(1.01\) per step, so the radius grows by \(\sqrt{1.01^{10}}=1.051\).

Exercise 2. Consider a problem with \(p_\theta(y^\star\mid x)=0.05\). By what factor does the likelihood gradient exceed the RL gradient on this problem?

Show solution

\(1/p=20\).
