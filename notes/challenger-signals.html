What Should the Challenger Make? Learnability, Proposer Rewards and Noisy Labels

Date: November 6, 2026  |  Estimated Reading Time: 7 min  |  Author: Chandak Chakma

Why problems at the edge of the Solver’s ability carry the most learning signal, how Asymmetric Self-Play, PAIRED, Absolute Zero, R-Zero and SOAR reward their problem setters, and why response-dependent label noise is more harmful than random noise.

9 Learnability

Key resultthe \(p(1-p)\) law

For a binary reward with success probability \(p\), the variance is \(\operatorname{Var}[R]=p(1-p)\), which attains its maximum (\(1/4\)) at \(p=1/2\). A GRPO group of size \(G\) provides a learning signal only if its rewards are not all equal, so the probability of an informative group is \[\Pr[\text{informative group}]=1-p^G-(1-p)^G .\]

Example\(G=8\)
\(p\) (or \(1-p\)) \(0.5\) \(0.9\) \(0.99\)
fraction of informative groups \(0.992\) \(0.570\) \(0.077\)

A problem that the Solver solves \(99\%\) of the time yields a learning signal in only about \(1\) group in \(13\). This arithmetic underlies the familiar prescription to “generate problems at the edge of the Solver’s ability”. R-Zero motivates the same target through a lower bound on the Solver’s remaining learning potential, which increases with \(\hat p(1-\hat p)\).

Pitfall

The law concerns the true success rate. In label-free self-play, we observe only the agreement rate \(\hat p\) with a pseudo-label, which coincides with \(p\) only when the pseudo-label is correct.

10 Proposer rewards: a comparison

Method Challenger (proposer) reward Ground truth from Main limitation
Asymmetric self-play (2017) \(r_A=\gamma\max(0,\,t_B-t_A)\), \(r_B=-\gamma t_B\) (completion times) environment requires a reversible environment
PAIRED (2020) regret \(=U(\pi_{\mathrm{antagonist}})-U(\pi_{\mathrm{protagonist}})\) environment reward requires a strong antagonist
Absolute Zero (2025) \(1-\bar r\) if \(0<\bar r<1\), else \(0\) (\(\bar r\) = solve rate) Python executor restricted to code
R-Zero (2025) \(\max\!\big(0,\ 1-2|\hat p-\tfrac12|-\lambda|C_k|/B\big)\) none (majority vote) pseudo-label accuracy decays
SOAR (2026) \(\mathrm{Perf}_H(\mathrm{Train}(\theta,D_\phi))-\mathrm{Perf}_H(\theta)\) labeled hard set \(H\) requires labels on \(H\)
Learning progress (2007) reduction of the learner’s error over time environment requires a measurable error

SOAR (Sundaram, Kempe and Ollivier, ICML 2026) is the current peer-reviewed frontier. Its formulation is bilevel: the teacher generates a problem set \(D_\phi\), the student trains on it briefly, and the teacher is rewarded by the student’s measured improvement on hard problems that the student initially could not solve (\(0\) of \(128\) samples correct). The paper finds that grounded rewards outperform learnability rewards, and that the well-posedness of generated questions matters more than the correctness of their answers. Its principal limitation is the labeled hard set \(H\). PowerPlay (Schmidhuber, 2011) is the conceptual precursor: it continually searches for the simplest problem that the current solver cannot yet solve.

Examplea blind spot in R-Zero’s reward

Suppose the Solver produces ten answers: four give \(20\), four give \(22\), and two contain no boxed answer. Then \(\hat p=4/10\) and \(r_{\mathrm{unc}}=1-2|0.4-0.5|=0.8\), a high reward, and the question passes the \(3\)–\(7\) filter. In the released code, the pseudo-label is whichever of \(20\) and \(22\) appeared first (Python’s max returns the first maximal key), and each unboxed answer is counted as a vote for the string "None"; the question is discarded later only if "None" wins the vote. The reward therefore cannot distinguish a two-way split from scattered guessing, and the choice between the two candidates is effectively arbitrary. Reported behavior should be checked against the released code.

Why it matters

Rewards constructed solely from the Solver’s agreement measure difficulty rather than correctness. Rewards grounded in genuine progress (SOAR, PAIRED) require a source of ground truth. Obtaining a grounded signal without labels remains the central open problem.

11 Learning from noisy labels

Key resultrandom noise only attenuates the signal

Suppose the observed reward \(\tilde R\) flips the true reward with probability \(\eta\), independently of the sampled response. Then \(\mathbb{E}[\tilde R\mid y]=\eta+(1-2\eta)R(y)\), and since \(\mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta]=0\), \[\mathbb{E}\big[\tilde R\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta\big]=(1-2\eta)\,\nabla_\theta J .\] Whenever \(\eta<1/2\), the expected gradient retains its direction; only its magnitude is reduced.

Key resultcorrelated noise biases the signal

If the label error depends on the response, we write \(\tilde R=R+\delta(x,y)\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[\tilde R\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta]=\nabla_\theta J+\mathbb{E}\big[\delta\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta\big]\), where the second term is a systematic drift toward whichever responses \(\delta\) favors.

Examplethe majority-vote reward

The pseudo-label is the mode \(m(x)\) of the Solver’s own samples, and the reward is \(\mathbf{1}[\text{answer}(y)=m(x)]\). Its expected value is \(\pi_\theta(m\mid x)\), which is maximized by placing all probability mass on \(m\), regardless of whether \(m\) is correct. For instance, let \(\pi(20)=0.40\), \(\pi(22)=0.35\), with the remaining answers sharing \(0.25\), and let the correct answer be \(22\). The vote selects \(20\) as the pseudo-label, and training drives \(\pi(20)\) toward \(1\): the model converges to a confident but incorrect answer.

Why it matters

This mechanism explains the characteristic “rise, then fall” pattern. Early in training the mode is usually correct (R-Zero’s pseudo-labels are \(79\%\) accurate in round 1), so sharpening is beneficial. As the Challenger generates harder questions, the mode is incorrect more often (\(63\%\) accurate by round 3), and the same sharpening begins to reinforce errors.

Exercises

Exercise 5. For \(G=4\) and \(p=0.75\), what fraction of groups is informative?

Show solution

\(1-0.75^4-0.25^4=1-0.316-0.004=0.680\).

Exercise 6. Under symmetric label noise with \(\eta=0.3\), by what factor is the expected gradient scaled? What happens at \(\eta=0.5\)?

Show solution

\(1-2\eta=0.4\). At \(\eta=0.5\) the factor is \(0\), so the labels carry no signal.
